Learning abstract actions for discrete planning over continuous control

From random walks alone, CSCG learns a map and FTN learns the movement policies that let a discrete planner navigate a continuous world.

Turning experience into a map and a repertoire

Astera hosts Dileep George’s work on Clone-Structured Causal Graphs (CSCG): models that learn to distinguish places that look alike by using the sequence of observations leading to them. This makes discrete planning possible in partially observed worlds. Here, I paired CSCG with FTN to extend that ability to continuous control.

The starting material is just a collection of blind random walks. K-means groups their first-person images into observation categories, and CSCG learns a graph from the transitions between those categories, using temporal context to separate otherwise ambiguous states. This graph is learned without action labels. It describes where the agent can go; FTN supplies the movements needed to get there.

For each destination node, we collect segments of the original walks that reach it from nearby nodes. Through behavior cloning, FTN learns these segments as an option: a small movement policy, or abstract action, for reaching that destination. A shared network holds the repertoire, with a different neural assembly activating each option. This follows the same segment-based approach used in the robotic arm experiments.

Planning with learned movements

A planner chooses a shortest path through the graph to a randomly selected goal. The agent activates the FTN mask for the next destination along that path, maintains that assembly while moving toward it, and switches options as it advances. CSCG tracks where the agent is, allowing the route to be revised during execution.

The movements in these demonstrations are noisy because all model fitting and policy training use only the initial random walks. There are no expert navigation demonstrations or additional training interactions to improve the options. Learning is entirely offline and off-policy; even so, the learned assemblies can be composed into purposeful navigation.

A modestly sized FTN thus holds a large repertoire of micro-policies that connects discrete planning to continuous movement. Combined with FTN’s demonstrated capacity for continual learning, this provides a prototype for open-world explorers that can expand their graph, learn new options, and plan with an accumulating repertoire of abilities.
